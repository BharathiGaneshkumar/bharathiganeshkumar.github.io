Adversarial ML & XAI

From Attacks to Insights: XAI and Defense in Network Security

Python MLP SHAP LIME ART UNSW-NB15

The problem

Deep learning models are black boxes. We often can’t explain why they reach a decision, and that lack of explainability is exactly why people are wary of trusting them in critical domains. One of those domains is network intrusion detection. A network intrusion detection system, or NIDS, watches traffic flowing through a network and flags which connections look malicious. Attackers exploit these models the same way they exploit any deep learning system: by adding small, carefully chosen perturbations to the input signal, changes too small for a human to notice but large enough to flip the model’s prediction. If an attack gets misclassified as benign when it’s actually malicious, the entire infrastructure behind that network is exposed.

The approach

This research trained a Multi-Layer Perceptron on UNSW-NB15, a standard benchmark of real network traffic, reaching 94.23% accuracy on clean data. Against that model, the Zeroth Order Optimization attack, or ZOO, was used: a black-box method that only needs the model’s predictions, not its internals, to find perturbations that fool it. Under attack, accuracy dropped to 67.8%. SHAP and LIME were then used to explain the model’s behavior before and after the attack, and comparing the two showed exactly which features it was exploiting: state-to-state latency and long-term connection patterns between source and destination. That gave a concrete target for defense: adversarial training, retraining the model on the adversarial examples themselves so it learns to resist that specific kind of perturbation. Accuracy recovered to 87.68%.

The result

It didn’t fully recover to 94%, and that gap is expected: there’s a real tradeoff between accuracy and robustness, since a model that’s harder to fool also gets slightly more cautious on clean data. The bigger takeaway was the loop itself: an attack reveals a vulnerability, explainability shows exactly where it lives, and that insight is what actually shapes an effective defense, rather than defending blindly.

OCULANG

Empowering Communication through Blink Language Detection

Python OpenCV Dlib (68-point) Django

The problem

People with neurodegenerative disorders and severe motor disabilities often lose the ability to speak or type, but their cognitive function stays intact and their eyes keep working. OCULANG is a real-time system that reads what a person needs directly from their eye movements. It builds on Blink to Speak, an eye sign language developed by Asha Ek Hope, the world’s first of its kind, a set of 50 commands assigned to basic eye actions so patients with ALS, spinal cord injuries, and other neurodegenerative conditions can communicate. Even with a language like that available, caregivers still need a fast, reliable way to actually read and interpret it in the moment.

The approach

OCULANG detects six basic eye movements: right, left, up, down, wink, and blink, and interprets combinations of them as messages in real time. Each video frame passes through Dlib’s pretrained facial landmark detector to locate 68 points on the face, isolating the eye regions specifically: landmarks 36 to 41 for the left eye, 42 to 47 for the right. OpenCV then masks out everything but the eyes and calculates the eye aspect ratio, the relative distance between the upper and lower eyelids, to detect blinks and winks, along with a horizontal gaze ratio based on how light and dark pixels distribute across the eye to detect left and right gaze. A decision-making algorithm maps the detected sequence of gestures to a predefined message, running through a Django interface that supports both live video and pre-recorded uploads.

The challenge

Detecting a gesture in a lab video is one thing. Every person’s eyes are shaped slightly differently, lighting in a real room isn’t consistent, and someone lying in a hospital bed isn’t going to hold perfectly still for a camera. None of that shows up in a clean, controlled dataset. Getting OCULANG to hold up against that variability, not just perform well in a demo, was the actual engineering problem, and it’s the one that mattered most, since a communication tool that only works in ideal conditions doesn’t help anyone who actually needs it.

The result

Evaluated on recorded eye-movement datasets, the system reliably recognized blinks, winks, and gaze direction, reaching 85.71% accuracy on message detection.

Active Thesis Research

MASS CUDA

Optimizing Multi-GPU MASS CUDA: Communication, Coordination, and Execution Performance

CUDA C++ NCCL UVA Clang/LLVM Nsight Compute

The framework

MASS CUDA is a general-purpose library for GPU-accelerated agent-based modeling, developed in the Distributed Systems Laboratory at UW Bothell, where I work under Prof. Munehiro Fukuda. Agent-based models simulate large numbers of independent agents moving and interacting inside a shared space, useful for anything from wildfire spread to disease modeling to cellular automata like Conway’s Game of Life. The library supports both single-GPU and multi-GPU execution. On the multi-GPU side, it supports two communication strategies: NVLink’s unified virtual addressing (UVA) and NVIDIA’s Collective Communication Library (NCCL). My thesis research builds on the multi-GPU side of the library, focused on performance optimization, and includes work on its Clang/LLVM-based translator.

The benchmarks

Four existing simulations exist in the library: Heat2D and GameOfLife, both cellular automaton-style simulations, and SugarScape and Tuberculosis, both agent-based simulations involving moving, interacting agents.
